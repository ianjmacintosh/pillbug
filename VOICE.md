# Ian's Writing Voice

## Core posture: fellow traveler, not expert

The consistent framing across all articles is "here's what I figured out, maybe it saves you time" — not "here's the correct way." He positions himself as someone who just solved a problem and is sharing the result, not as an authority. "I'd love it if you'd go take a look at what I made." "Maybe reading this article will save you some time if you're in a similar position." "For whatever it's worth" and "I personally" are recurring markers of perspective-sharing rather than decree-issuing.

## Sentence rhythm

Short declarative sentences carry the punch; longer sentences carry the explanation. The contrast is deliberate and consistent:

- "This sucks. This is a world without domain names."
- "Shippable? You bet. Perfect? Nope."
- "Beats me."
- "Don't do that."
- "That fixed it for me!"

These short beats appear after longer passages to reset the pace, land a point, or signal a decision. The rhythm feels like spoken thought — not clipped, not dense.

## Register code-switching

He moves fluidly between formal and casual within the same piece, sometimes within the same sentence. Technical terms ("risk-limiting audits," "ballot-marking devices," "throughput") sit next to "yippee!", "booo", "stoked", "crapshoot", and "some guy driving around my neighborhood in a van." This isn't inconsistency — it's a deliberate signal that he's comfortable in technical or professional contexts but resistant to corporate-speak or academic register for its own sake.

## Self-deprecation and emotional honesty

The humor is self-directed and specific. He names his own bad decisions plainly:

- "Making Stupid-Big Components" (section header for his own code)
- "jumping around all mimbledy-bimbledy from search result to search result"
- "a gajillion npm deps"
- "I didn't even know how to write unit tests"

He's also willing to be genuinely vulnerable — recounting sleeping on a stranger's lawn in the travel article without softening it, then pivoting to the lesson.

## Admitting ignorance plainly

"Beats me." Full stop. No hedging construction around it, no apology. He'll also write "I haven't found a satisfying answer" or "I'm personally hesitant" on topics where he has real opinions, because he distinguishes between having a view and having certainty.

## Direct imperatives

He tells readers what to do without cushioning it into a suggestion: "Go back to the NCSL's website and review your own favorite state's policies." "Don't watch the world through your iPhone." "Don't do that." The directness reads as respect for the reader's time, not bossiness.

## Concrete metaphors over abstraction

When explaining something abstract, he reaches for a tangible analogy and commits to it. The gym analogy for online learning platforms runs through an entire article. The banking analogy for election irreversibility closes a piece about voting systems. The metaphors aren't decorative — they're load-bearing.

## Absurdly specific examples

The specificity is often comedic. "Some guy driving around my neighborhood in a van." "The world's greatest network engineer intentionally trying to make it worse." "An animated 'Deal With It' dog-wearing-sunglasses GIF from 2010." The precision signals that he's drawing from actual experience or actual mental images, not generic placeholders.

## Pop culture as tension deflator

Uses a pop culture reference to lower the stakes on a serious topic — a Ray Liotta Goodfellas reference when explaining ballot harvesting, the Deal With It GIF when describing stakeholder resistance. Not trying to be funny exactly; using the familiar to make the unfamiliar less threatening.

## Parenthetical asides

Parentheticals are a genuine thinking-out-loud habit, not a rhetorical device. "(maybe to print out airplane tickets)" "(paying/forcing people to vote a certain way)" "(which will probably be your 'second factor' for authentication)" — tangential clarifications that feel like he's still working it out as he writes.

## Failure-first explanation

A recurring structure: try the wrong thing → explain _why_ it was wrong at a conceptual level → move to what actually works. The failure explanation does real teaching. He doesn't just say "X didn't work" — he says "X can't work because of Y," which makes the correct fix make sense. The mobile footers article (leading with `env(safe-area-inset-bottom)`) and the HSTS article (leading with trying to pass headers through Cloudflare) are the clearest examples.

## Meta-commentary on his own writing

Occasionally steps outside the piece to comment on it. "Probably, it's pretty long!" He's not above acknowledging that he's given you a lot to read.

## What he avoids

- Warm-up paragraphs that restate the title
- Corporate-speak and management-consultant phrasing
- Superlatives and buzzwords
- Performative neutrality — opinions are stated plainly, not hedged into mush
- Condescension toward less experienced readers
- Pretending to have certainty he doesn't have
- Generic calls to action at the end

## Emojis

Used in some articles (especially earlier or tutorial-style ones) as tonal markers, not decoration. 🍾 for a successful result, ⌨️ to signal try-this-yourself. Not present in all articles — appears to be a choice based on article type or era.
